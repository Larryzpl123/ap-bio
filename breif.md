### 2. Stages of Cellular Respiration: Locations, Inputs, Outputs

**Aerobic respiration** (eukaryotic cells):
1. **Glycolysis**  
   - Location: Cytoplasm  
   - Inputs: 1 glucose (6C), 2 NAD⁺, 2 ATP  
   - Outputs: 2 pyruvate (3C), 2 ATP (net), 2 NADH

2. **Pyruvate oxidation** (link reaction)  
   - Location: Mitochondrial matrix  
   - Inputs: 2 pyruvate, 2 CoA, 2 NAD⁺  
   - Outputs: 2 acetyl‑CoA, 2 CO₂, 2 NADH

3. **Citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle)**  
   - Location: Mitochondrial matrix  
   - Inputs (per turn): acetyl‑CoA, 3 NAD⁺, 1 FAD, 1 ADP + Pᵢ  
   - Outputs (per turn): 2 CO₂, 3 NADH, 1 FADH₂, 1 ATP (GTP)  
   - The cycle turns **twice** per glucose.

4. **Oxidative phosphorylation** (electron transport chain + chemiosmosis)  
   - Location: Inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae)  
   - Inputs: NADH, FADH₂, O₂, ADP + Pᵢ  
   - Outputs: H₂O, ~26–28 ATP (bulk of ATP), NAD⁺, FAD

**Fermentation (anaerobic):**
- Glycolysis still produces 2 ATP + 2 NADH.
- NADH donates electrons to pyruvate (or derivative) to regenerate NAD⁺.
- Alcoholic fermentation: pyruvate → ethanol + CO₂
- Lactic acid fermentation: pyruvate → lactate

### 3. Photosynthesis: Light Reactions vs Calvin Cycle

| Feature | Light‑dependent reactions | Calvin cycle (light‑independent) |
|---------|----------------------------|-----------------------------------|
| **Location** | Thylakoid membrane (chloroplast) | Stroma (chloroplast) |
| **Inputs** | H₂O, light energy, NADP⁺, ADP + Pᵢ | CO₂, ATP, NADPH |
| **Outputs** | O₂ (waste), ATP, NADPH | G3P → glucose (and other sugars), ADP + Pᵢ, NADP⁺ |
| **Purpose** | Convert light energy → chemical energy (ATP, NADPH); split H₂O | Use ATP, NADPH to fix CO₂ into organic molecules (carbon fixation) |
| **Key steps** | PSII, electron transport chain, PSI, chemiosmosis (ATP synthase) | Carbon fixation (RuBisCO), reduction, regeneration of RuBP |

### 4. Endosymbiotic Theory + 4 Evidence Points

**Theory:** Mitochondria and chloroplasts were once free‑living prokaryotes that were engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell and established a mutually beneficial relationship.

**4 Major lines of evidence:**
1. **Size & structure** – Similar in size to bacteria; double membranes (outer from host, inner from prokaryote).
2. **Genetic material** – Contain their own circular DNA (without histones), like bacterial chromosomes.
3. **Replication** – Reproduce by binary fission, independently of the host cell cycle.
4. **Ribosomes & antibiotics** – Have 70S ribosomes (same as prokaryotes) and are sensitive to antibiotics that target bacterial ribosomes (e.g., chloramphenicol).

### 5. Cell Signalling: Reception → Transduction → Response

**Three stages:**
1. **Reception** – A signalling molecule (ligand) binds to a specific receptor protein (often on cell surface or inside the cell). Receptor changes shape.
2. **Transduction** – The signal is relayed through a cascade of molecular interactions, often involving phosphorylation by kinases and second messengers (cAMP, Ca²⁺). Each step amplifies the signal.
3. **Response** – The final effector is activated, leading to a cellular response (e.g., gene expression on/off, enzyme activation, apoptosis).

**Types of receptors:** G‑protein‑coupled receptors (GPCRs), receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), ligand‑gated ion channels, intracellular receptors.

---

### 6. Negative vs Positive Feedback (with Examples)

- **Negative feedback:** The response **reduces** the initial stimulus, maintaining homeostasis.
  - *Examples:* Thermoregulation (sweating, shivering); blood glucose regulation (insulin/glucagon); thyroxine regulation (TRH‑TSH‑thyroid hormone).
- **Positive feedback:** The response **amplifies** the initial stimulus, driving the process to completion.
  - *Examples:* Oxytocin during childbirth (contractions → more oxytocin); blood clotting cascade; ripening of fruit (ethylene).

### 7. Mitosis vs Meiosis Comparison

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---------|---------|---------|
| **Purpose** | Growth, repair, asexual reproduction | Production of gametes (sexual reproduction) |
| **Number of divisions** | 1 | 2 (Meiosis I and II) |
| **Starting cell** | Diploid (2n) | Diploid (2n) |
| **Daughter cells** | 2 genetically identical diploid cells | 4 genetically unique haploid cells (n) |
| **Synapsis / crossing over** | No | Yes, in prophase I |
| **Homologous pairs** | Do not pair | Pair and segregate in anaphase I |
| **Variation** | None (clones; mutations aside) | High due to crossing over, independent assortment, random fertilisation |
| **DNA replication** | Once, before division | Once, before Meiosis I |

### 8. Hardy‑Weinberg Conditions (5) and Equations

**Equations:**
- Allele frequencies: \( p + q = 1 \)
  - \( p \) = frequency of dominant allele, \( q \) = frequency of recessive allele.
- Genotype frequencies: \( p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1 \)
  - \( p^2 \) = homozygous dominant, \( 2pq \) = heterozygous, \( q^2 \) = homozygous recessive.

**5 conditions** (none may occur if evolution is happening):
1. **Very large population** (no genetic drift).
2. **No gene flow** (no migration in/out).
3. **No mutations** (no new alleles introduced).
4. **Random mating** (no sexual selection).
5. **No natural selection** (all genotypes have equal fitness).

When all conditions are met, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant from generation to generation.

### 9. Chi‑Square Procedure + Interpretation

**Used to:** Determine if observed data differ significantly from expected values (goodness‑of‑fit test), often in genetics.

**Formula:**  
\( \chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E} \)  
where \( O \) = observed count, \( E \) = expected count.

**Procedure:**
1. State null hypothesis (H₀) – “no difference between observed and expected.”
2. Calculate expected values based on the model (e.g., Mendelian ratios).
3. Compute chi‑square statistic.
4. Determine degrees of freedom (\( df = \text{number of categories} - 1\)).
5. Compare calculated χ² to critical value from table at chosen significance level (typically α = 0.05).
6. If χ² > critical value → **reject H₀**, data significantly different from expected.  
   If χ² ≤ critical value → **fail to reject H₀**, differences due to chance likely.

### 10. Natural Selection’s 4 Conditions

1. **Variation** – Individuals in a population differ in their traits.
2. **Inheritance** – These variations are heritable (passed to offspring).
3. **Differential survival/reproduction** – More individuals are produced than can survive; some variants survive and reproduce more successfully.
4. **Non‑random** – Survival and reproduction are not random but influenced by the traits.

Result: allele frequencies in the population change over generations (evolution by natural selection).

---

### 11. Types of Selection (Directional, Stabilizing, Disruptive)

- **Directional selection:** Favours one extreme phenotype, shifting the population mean in one direction.  
  *Example:* Peppered moth colour during Industrial Revolution.
- **Stabilizing selection:** Favours intermediate phenotypes; reduces variation around the mean.  
  *Example:* Human birth weight (very small or large babies have lower survival).
- **Disruptive selection:** Favours both extreme phenotypes over the intermediate, potentially leading to speciation.  
  *Example:* African seedcracker birds with very small or very large beaks (intermediate beaks cannot crack common seeds).

### 12. Reading Phylogenetic Trees

- A phylogenetic tree (cladogram) shows evolutionary relationships.
- **Tips (leaves):** Living (or extant) species / taxa.
- **Nodes:** Represent a common ancestor; a speciation event.
- **Branches:** Represent lineages evolving over time.
- **Root:** Common ancestor of all taxa in the tree.
- **Sister taxa:** Groups that share an immediate common ancestor; they are each other’s closest relatives.
- **Monophyletic group (clade):** Includes an ancestor and ALL of its descendants.
- **Outgroup:** A taxon known to have diverged earlier; helps root the tree.
- Reading a tree: Relative order of branching indicates relatedness; time may or may not be represented by branch length. Do NOT read closeness at the tips as more closely related unless they share a more recent common ancestor.

### 13. Carbon and Nitrogen Cycle Key Steps

**Carbon cycle:**
- Photosynthesis – plants fix CO₂ into organic compounds.
- Respiration – all organisms return CO₂ to atmosphere.
- Decomposition – decomposers release CO₂ and deposit organic matter.
- Combustion – burning of fossil fuels and biomass releases CO₂.
- Ocean exchange – CO₂ dissolves in water, forms carbonates; marine organisms use for shells.
- Sedimentation – formation of fossil fuels / limestone over long periods.

**Nitrogen cycle:**
- **Nitrogen fixation:** N₂ → NH₃ (ammonia) by nitrogen‑fixing bacteria (free‑living or in root nodules, e.g., *Rhizobium*). Also lightning and industrial Haber process.
- **Nitrification:** NH₃ → NO₂⁻ (nitrite) by *Nitrosomonas* → NO₃⁻ (nitrate) by *Nitrobacter*.
- **Assimilation:** Plants absorb NO₃⁻ (or NH₄⁺) to make amino acids, nucleic acids.
- **Ammonification:** Decomposers break down organic nitrogen from dead organisms, releasing NH₄⁺.
- **Denitrification:** NO₃⁻ → N₂ gas by denitrifying bacteria, returning nitrogen to atmosphere.

### 14. r vs K Selection

Two ends of a reproductive strategy spectrum:

| Trait | r‑selected species | K‑selected species |
|-------|--------------------|---------------------|
| **Environment** | Unstable, unpredictable | Stable, near carrying capacity |
| **Population size** | Often well below carrying capacity | Often near carrying capacity |
| **Life span** | Short | Long |
| **Maturation** | Rapid | Slow |
| **Reproduction** | Many offspring, early, once (semelparous) | Few offspring, late, repeated (iteroparous) |
| **Parental care** | Little or none | Extensive |
| **Offspring size** | Small | Large |
| **Examples** | Insects, weeds, bacteria | Elephants, whales, humans |

### 15. Lac vs trp Operon

| Feature | **lac operon** | **trp operon** |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Type** | Inducible (usually off, can be turned on) | Repressible (usually on, can be turned off) |
| **Function** | Catabolic: breaks down lactose | Anabolic: synthesises tryptophan |
| **Regulatory gene** | *lacI* produces **repressor** protein | *trpR* produces **repressor** protein |
| **Effector** | Allolactose (inducer) inactivates repressor | Tryptophan (co‑repressor) activates repressor |
| **When active** | Lactose present, glucose low (cAMP‑CAP helps) | Tryptophan absent → repressor inactive → transcription ON |
| **When repressed** | No lactose → repressor bound to operator | Tryptophan present → repressor binds operator, transcription OFF |

Note: both operons have a promoter, operator, and structural genes; lac operon also has CAP binding site for catabolite repression.
